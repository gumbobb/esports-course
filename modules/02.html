Week 2 of 15 · Unit: The Industry

Industry Structure: Who Pays and Why

Publishers, leagues, teams, platforms, and sponsors, and the money that moves between them.

Why this week mattersEvery program, event, and career decision later in the course is a money decision underneath. This week you learn to read the business model.

This week you will be able to

1. Explain the five main revenue streams in esports (sponsorship, media rights, publisher fees, merchandise and tickets, digital goods) and estimate their relative size.
2. Compare the publisher-controlled model of esports with the league-owned model of traditional sports and identify one advantage and one risk of each.
3. Analyze a real team or league's business and identify its primary revenue source and its biggest cost.

Supports course outcomes: Outcome 1

Learn

Read (about 15 minutes)

Follow the money and the industry makes sense. Sponsorship and advertising are the largest revenue source by a wide margin: brands pay to put their names in front of a young, hard-to-reach audience. Media rights (platforms paying for exclusive broadcast) grew fast and then shrank when exclusive deals stopped paying off. Publisher fees and investment matter because the companies that own the games often fund the leagues around them. Tickets and merchandise are small compared to traditional sports because most fans watch online. Digital goods, like in-game skins tied to teams or events, have become a major source in some titles.

The biggest structural fact is publisher control. The NBA does not own basketball, but Riot Games owns League of Legends and Valorant, and it can change the rules, the format, the revenue split, or the game itself at any time. That gives esports speed and consistency but creates risk: a publisher can shut down a competitive scene, as has happened more than once. Teams and organizers live with that dependency and negotiate around it.

Teams are businesses with thin margins. Their costs are salaries, housing or facilities, travel, staff, and content production. Their revenue is sponsorship first, prize money a distant second, and increasingly content and merchandise. Many well-known organizations have lost money for years; the ones that survive diversify into content creation, apparel, and events. Scholastic programs run on a different model entirely: school budgets, booster clubs, grants, and small local sponsors, with no prize money to speak of. Weeks 3 and 12 return to that.

When you look at any esports organization this week, ask three questions: What does it sell? Who buys it? What happens if the publisher changes its mind?

Check your understanding

A short, ungraded five-question check in Google Forms. Take it as many times as you like; you will see the correct answer and a short explanation after each question.

Questions in this week's check
  1. Which revenue stream is largest for most esports organizations?
  2. What is the main risk created by publisher control of a game's competitive scene?
  3. Why are ticket and merchandise sales smaller in esports than in traditional sports?
  4. Give one reason a team might invest in content creation.
  5. How does a scholastic program's funding model differ from a professional team's?
Open the Week 2 check (Google Forms)

Do: Revenue Pie

Individual, data 40 minutes

Purpose: Turn the revenue streams from the reading into a visual estimate for one real organization so you practice reading a business model.

  1. Choose one professional team, league, or tournament organizer.
  2. Find at least two sources on how it makes money (news articles, interviews, annual reports, Liquipedia).
  3. Estimate the share of each revenue stream as a pie chart. Estimates are fine; label them as estimates.
  4. List its two largest costs.
  5. Submit the chart with your sources through the Week 2 form.
ChoicePick a professional organization, a collegiate program with public budget information, or a tournament organizer. Any is fine as long as you can find two sources.

Discuss

Padlet discussion Post by Wednesday, two replies by Friday

Prompt: Publisher control: good or bad for the industry? Argue one side using a specific example of a publisher decision (a rule change, a league launch, a scene shutdown). Then reply to someone who argued the other side.

A good post is 5 to 8 sentences, takes a position, and uses something from this week's reading or video. A good reply asks a question or adds an example; "I agree" does not count. Posts can be text, a 60-second voice note, or a short video.

Assess: Business Model Brief

Individual written or recorded 25 points Due Saturday 11:59 PM

A one-page brief (or 3-minute recorded explanation) on one esports organization: what it sells, who buys it, its largest revenue source, its largest cost, its biggest risk, and one recommendation you would make to its leadership. Use at least three sources.

Submit as: a one-page document, a slide, or a captioned 3-minute video. Share the file or link through the Week 2 submission form.
