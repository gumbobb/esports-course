Week 3 of 15 · Unit: Programs and Teams

Building a Scholastic Esports Program

Mission, structure, facilities, equipment, and the first conversations with a school.

Why this week mattersMost esports careers in education start with one person building a program from nothing. This week is the blueprint, and it is also the first piece of your capstone.

This week you will be able to

1. Write a mission statement and three measurable goals for a scholastic esports program.
2. Design an organizational structure for a program (adviser, coaches, captains, support roles) and justify each role.
3. Create a realistic equipment and facilities list for a starter program with estimated costs.

Supports course outcomes: Outcomes 2 and 6

Learn

Read (about 15 minutes)

A scholastic esports program is a school activity first and a gaming activity second. The programs that last are the ones that can answer a principal's questions: What will students learn? How is it supervised? What does it cost? How does it connect to academics and careers? Your mission statement answers the first question. Good missions name the students served and the outcomes (teamwork, communication, career exploration, belonging), not the games.

Structure comes next. The minimum is a faculty adviser who is accountable to the school, a coach for each title (often a student or volunteer at first), team captains, and support roles that are the real secret of a strong program: broadcast crew, social media, analysts, event staff. Support roles are how a program serves fifty students with twelve competitive slots. NASEF's model of 'beyond the game' roles is the standard reference.

Equipment is where new advisers overspend. A starter program needs reliable machines that meet the minimum specs of its chosen titles, a stable wired internet connection (the most common failure point in school esports), peripherals, headsets with microphones, and a space with power, seating, and supervision sightlines. Consoles and Chromebook-friendly titles lower the cost dramatically. Facilities can be an existing lab after school; dedicated arenas come later, if ever.

The first conversations with administration are a pitch: mission, supervision plan, budget, and a connection to something the school already values (CTE pathways, attendance, belonging for students who are not in other activities). Week 12 returns to funding; this week you build the thing you will fund.

Check your understanding

A short, ungraded five-question check in Google Forms. Take it as many times as you like; you will see the correct answer and a short explanation after each question.

Questions in this week's check
  1. What should a scholastic esports mission statement name instead of games?
  2. Why are support roles (broadcast, analysis, social media) important to a program's reach?
  3. What is the most common technical failure point in school esports?
  4. Name two ways to lower a starter program's equipment cost.
  5. What question is a principal most likely to ask first, and how does the program answer it?
Open the Week 3 check (Google Forms)

Do: Program Org Chart

Individual, visual 45 minutes

Purpose: Design the people structure before the equipment, because roles determine what a program can do and how many students it serves.

  1. Decide the scale: one title or three, 15 students or 50.
  2. Draw an org chart with adviser, coaches, captains, and at least three support roles.
  3. Under each role write one sentence: what they do and who they report to.
  4. Mark which roles could be filled by students on day one.
  5. Submit the chart through the Week 3 form.
ChoiceDesign for a school like yours, a school with no budget, or a large program with multiple titles. Any scale is fine; the chart has to match the scale.

Discuss

Padlet discussion Post by Wednesday, two replies by Friday

Prompt: Should a scholastic program be competitive first (win matches) or participation first (serve as many students as possible)? Pick one, defend it, and describe one tradeoff you would accept.

A good post is 5 to 8 sentences, takes a position, and uses something from this week's reading or video. A good reply asks a question or adds an example; "I agree" does not count. Posts can be text, a 60-second voice note, or a short video.

Assess: Capstone Checkpoint 1: Concept and Audience

Capstone piece 30 points Due Saturday 11:59 PM

One page that starts your capstone: your track (program or event), a title, the audience it serves, a mission statement, three measurable goals, and a paragraph on why it matters to that audience. Track A students also include a starter equipment list with estimated costs; Track B students include the game, format, and target participant count.

Submit as: a one-page document or a 5-slide deck. Share the file or link through the Week 3 submission form.
