Week 12 of 15 · Unit: Business and Ethics

Sponsorship and Funding

Proposals, partnerships, grants, fundraising, and asking for money without apologizing for it.

Why this week mattersPrograms and events run on money that someone has to ask for. This week you learn to ask well, and it is the third piece of your capstone.

This week you will be able to

1. Identify at least four funding sources for a scholastic program or event and the requirements of each.
2. Write a sponsorship proposal with tiered packages, audience data, and specific deliverables.
3. Build a one-year funding plan that combines at least three sources.

Supports course outcomes: Outcome 6

Learn

Read (about 15 minutes)

Funding comes from more places than most advisers realize. School sources: activity funds, CTE or technology budgets, PTA or booster clubs. Grants: foundations, technology companies, and esports organizations offer equipment and startup grants, often with simple applications. Fundraising: tournaments with entry fees, concessions, community game nights, merchandise. Sponsorship: local businesses, gaming retailers, internet providers, colleges with esports programs, and regional brands that want to reach students and families.

A sponsorship proposal is a trade, not a donation request. The sponsor gets something: logo on the broadcast overlay and jerseys, social media mentions with real audience numbers, naming rights for an event, a booth or presence at events, access to a community they want. You get money or equipment. Tiers (for example, Bronze, Silver, Gold with rising deliverables and prices) let a small business say yes at a level it can afford.

Audience data is what makes the ask credible. How many students in the program, how many families, how many viewers on the last broadcast, how many followers, what events are planned. Even small real numbers beat vague claims. Proposals that show a specific audience and specific deliverables get read; proposals that say 'support our team' get filed.

The ask itself: find the right person, lead with what is in it for them, be specific about the deliverables and the price, make saying yes easy, and follow up. After they say yes, deliver exactly what was promised and send a thank-you with evidence (a photo of the logo on the overlay). Sponsors who are thanked renew; sponsors who are forgotten do not.

Check your understanding

A short, ungraded five-question check in Google Forms. Take it as many times as you like; you will see the correct answer and a short explanation after each question.

Questions in this week's check
  1. Name four funding sources and one requirement of each.
  2. Why is a sponsorship a trade rather than a donation?
  3. What do tiered packages accomplish?
  4. What makes an ask credible?
  5. What should happen after a sponsor says yes?
Open the Week 12 check (Google Forms)

Do: Sponsor Prospect List

Individual, research 40 minutes

Purpose: Identify real potential sponsors so your proposal is written to someone, not to no one.

  1. List ten local or regional businesses or organizations that reach students and families.
  2. For each: why they would care, what tier fits them, and who to contact (role, not personal information).
  3. Rank the top three and write one sentence of the opening line you would use with each.
  4. Submit through the Week 12 form.
ChoiceResearch your own community or any community you know.

Discuss

Padlet discussion Post by Wednesday, two replies by Friday

Prompt: A fast-food chain offers your program a large sponsorship. A parent objects on health grounds. Do you take it? Decide, defend, and describe what conditions, if any, you would attach.

A good post is 5 to 8 sentences, takes a position, and uses something from this week's reading or video. A good reply asks a question or adds an example; "I agree" does not count. Posts can be text, a 60-second voice note, or a short video.

Assess: Capstone Checkpoint 3: Sponsorship Proposal and Funding Plan

Capstone piece 30 points Due Saturday 11:59 PM

A sponsorship proposal (two to four pages or 8 to 10 slides) for your capstone program or event: who you are, audience data, three tiers with prices and specific deliverables, and the ask. Plus a one-page funding plan combining at least three sources with amounts and timing for one year.

Submit as: a document or slide deck. Share the file or link through the Week 12 submission form.
