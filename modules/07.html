Week 7 of 15 · Unit: Programs and Teams

Scholastic Esports: Leagues, Rules, and Eligibility

NASEF, HSEL, PlayVS, state associations, and the rules that make school competition legitimate.

Why this week mattersSchool esports lives inside rules that professional esports does not: eligibility, academics, sanctioning, and age-appropriate titles. Knowing the landscape is what makes you credible to a school.

This week you will be able to

1. Compare the major scholastic esports organizations by cost, titles, structure, and educational support.
2. Explain how state high school athletic or activity associations sanction esports and what sanctioning changes for a program.
3. Apply eligibility and title-appropriateness rules to a sample roster and schedule.

Supports course outcomes: Outcomes 2 and 5

Learn

Read (about 15 minutes)

Scholastic esports grew up fast and in several directions at once. NASEF is a nonprofit focused on education: free curriculum, career exploration, and club support, with competition as one piece. HSEL is a national competitive league with a broad title list. PlayVS built a platform and partnered with many state activity associations, which is the route to 'varsity' status in a lot of states. Each has different costs, title lists, seasons, and expectations, and many schools use more than one.

Sanctioning by a state association matters. When esports is an official activity, programs get access to school funding structures, eligibility rules that mirror other sports (grades, attendance, conduct), coach training requirements, and the legitimacy that comes with a letter on a jacket. It also brings rules: approved title lists (often excluding mature-rated games), season windows, and paperwork. The tradeoff is worth understanding before you choose a league.

Title selection is where scholastic esports differs most from professional. Rating (ESRB), content, device access, and league approval all narrow the list. Rocket League, Super Smash Bros. Ultimate, Mario Kart, Overwatch 2, and Valorant are common; some titles are approved in one league and not another, and a school district may restrict further. Picking titles students want that the school will approve is a diplomacy skill.

Eligibility is the detail that catches new advisers. Grade requirements, attendance, transfer rules, and age limits vary by association and league, and a single ineligible player can forfeit a season. The habit to build: check eligibility before tryouts, document it, and re-check at every grading period.

Check your understanding

A short, ungraded five-question check in Google Forms. Take it as many times as you like; you will see the correct answer and a short explanation after each question.

Questions in this week's check
  1. Which organization is primarily education-focused with free curriculum?
  2. What does state sanctioning change for a program?
  3. Name two factors that narrow title selection for schools.
  4. Why should eligibility be re-checked each grading period?
  5. What is one tradeoff of joining a sanctioned league?
Open the Week 7 check (Google Forms)

Do: League Comparison Table

Individual, research 45 minutes

Purpose: Build the decision tool a program adviser actually needs when choosing where to compete.

  1. Create a table with NASEF, HSEL, PlayVS, and your state's association (or a neighboring state's).
  2. Fill in: cost, titles, season timing, device requirements, educational support, and sanctioning status.
  3. Add a row for 'best fit for' and describe the kind of program each suits.
  4. Write two sentences recommending one for a specific school scenario.
  5. Submit through the Week 7 form.
ChoiceResearch your own state or any state with a sanctioned program.

Discuss

Padlet discussion Post by Wednesday, two replies by Friday

Prompt: Mature-rated titles: should scholastic leagues allow games rated M when many of their players already play them at home? Take a side, use one league's actual policy as evidence, and consider the school's position.

A good post is 5 to 8 sentences, takes a position, and uses something from this week's reading or video. A good reply asks a question or adds an example; "I agree" does not count. Posts can be text, a 60-second voice note, or a short video.

Assess: Eligibility and Title Case Study

Individual case analysis 25 points Due Saturday 11:59 PM

You will receive a sample program (roster with grades and attendance, proposed title list, proposed schedule) in the submission form. Identify every eligibility and title problem using one real league's rules, explain each, and propose a corrected roster, title list, and schedule.

Submit as: a document or annotated table, or a captioned video walkthrough with a written list of findings. Share the file or link through the Week 7 submission form.
