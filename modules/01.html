Week 1 of 15 · Unit: The Industry

What Esports Is

Where competitive gaming came from, what counts as an esport, and who is in the room when a match happens.

Why this week mattersYou cannot manage what you cannot describe. This week gives you the vocabulary and the map of the ecosystem that every later week assumes.

This week you will be able to

1. Define esports and distinguish it from casual gaming and traditional sports using at least three criteria.
2. Trace the history of competitive gaming from arcade high-score contests to modern leagues in a timeline with at least six dated events.
3. Identify the main roles in the esports ecosystem (players, teams, publishers, leagues, platforms, sponsors, fans) and explain how each depends on the others.

Supports course outcomes: Outcome 1

Learn

Read (about 15 minutes)

Esports is organized, competitive video gaming with spectators. That short definition hides three things that make it different from a Friday night of gaming with friends. First, it is organized: there are rules, schedules, rosters, and officials. Second, it is competitive at a level where skill is measured, ranked, and rewarded. Third, it has an audience, and the audience is what turns a hobby into an industry. No audience, no sponsors, no broadcasts, no salaries.

The history is older than most people think. Stanford hosted a Spacewar tournament in 1972. Arcade high-score competitions filled the 1980s. The 1990s brought networked PC play and the first organized leagues around games like Quake and StarCraft, and South Korea built television channels around StarCraft in the early 2000s. Streaming changed everything after 2011, when Twitch made it possible for anyone to watch anyone. By the late 2010s, League of Legends World Championship finals drew tens of millions of viewers and filled stadiums.

The ecosystem has a predictable cast. Publishers own the games and therefore control the rules of competition, which is the single biggest difference from traditional sports: nobody owns basketball, but Riot Games owns League of Legends. Teams and organizations employ players and staff. Leagues and tournament organizers run competition. Platforms (Twitch, YouTube) carry the broadcast. Sponsors and advertisers pay for access to the audience. Fans supply attention, which is the real product being sold. Scholastic and collegiate programs sit alongside all of this, with schools as the organizations and students as the players.

As you read about this industry, keep one question in mind every week: who is paying, and what are they paying for? The answer explains almost every decision you will study.

Check your understanding

A short, ungraded five-question check in Google Forms. Take it as many times as you like; you will see the correct answer and a short explanation after each question.

Questions in this week's check
  1. Which of the following best distinguishes esports from casual gaming?
  2. Who controls the official rules of competition for a given esports title, and why?
  3. Place these in order: Twitch launch, Stanford Spacewar tournament, Korean StarCraft TV era, first LoL World Championship.
  4. Which group supplies the 'product' that sponsors actually pay for?
  5. Name one way scholastic esports differs from professional esports in who the organization is.

Do: Ecosystem Map

Individual, visual 45 minutes

Purpose: Make the ecosystem concrete by mapping it for one real game, so the abstract roles become specific companies and people you can look up.

  1. Pick one esports title you know or want to know (Rocket League, Valorant, Super Smash Bros., Overwatch, or another).
  2. Using Liquipedia and the publisher's site, identify the publisher, two major teams, one league or tournament, the main streaming platform, and two sponsors.
  3. Draw the map: boxes for each role, arrows showing who pays whom and who provides what. Paper and a photo, Google Drawings, Canva, or slides all work.
  4. Add one sentence under the map: what would break first if the audience disappeared?
  5. Upload the image or link through the Week 1 submission form.
ChoiceChoose any game. If you do not follow esports, pick one with a scholastic scene (Rocket League, Smash, Mario Kart) so the scholastic layer is easy to find.

Discuss

Padlet discussion Post by Wednesday, two replies by Friday

Prompt: Is esports a sport? Take a position and defend it with at least one criterion from the reading (organization, measured skill, audience) and one example. Then, more interesting: does the answer matter for how a school should treat an esports program?

A good post is 5 to 8 sentences, takes a position, and uses something from this week's reading or video. A good reply asks a question or adds an example; "I agree" does not count. Posts can be text, a 60-second voice note, or a short video.
